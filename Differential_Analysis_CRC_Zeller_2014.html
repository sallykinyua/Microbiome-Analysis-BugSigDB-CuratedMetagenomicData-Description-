Potential of Fecal Microbiota for Early-Stage Detection of Colorectal Cancer

A Differential Abundance Analysis of the ZellerG_2014 Cohort

Sally Karimi Kinyua

2026-08-12

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) develops gradually, typically progressing through a precursor lesion — the adenoma — before becoming invasive cancer. Several bacterial taxa have been proposed as biomarkers of this progression, but their consistency across disease stages is still an active area of research.

This analysis re-examines gut microbiome composition across three stages of that progression, using shotgun metagenomic data from Zeller et al., 2014, “Potential of fecal microbiota for early-stage detection of colorectal cancer,” published in Molecular Systems Biology.

Research question

Does gut microbial community composition differ between healthy controls, adenoma patients, and CRC patients — and if so, which specific taxa drive that difference?

Three groups are compared throughout:

Group Description n
control Healthy, tumour-free individuals 61
adenoma Colorectal adenoma (precursor lesion) 42
CRC Colorectal cancer 53

control is used as the reference group throughout, so every result below is framed as “adenoma vs. healthy” or “CRC vs. healthy.”

Analytical approach

Two complementary differential abundance methods are used:

  • ANCOM-BC2 — a compositionally-aware method that models bias directly and works on absolute count-scale data. Generally more statistically powered.
  • LEfSe (Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size) — combines non-parametric testing (Kruskal-Wallis, Wilcoxon) with linear discriminant analysis to rank taxa by effect size. More conservative, widely used for its interpretable effect-size ranking.

Agreement between the two methods is treated as stronger evidence for a given taxon’s association with disease status.

Downloading abundance data

curatedMetagenomicData() can return data on two different scales, controlled by the counts argument. Both are needed here, since the two downstream methods expect different input scales:

  • counts = FALSE → true relative abundance (proportions, summing to ~100 per sample) — used for quality control, diversity, and LEfSe
  • counts = TRUE → estimated read counts (scaled by sequencing depth) — used for ANCOM-BC2, which models count-based bias directly

Quality Control & Community Structure

Sequencing depth consistency

Before testing for biological differences, it’s important to confirm there’s no systematic technical bias between groups — for example, one group being sequenced to a different depth than another.

colData(single_tse_ra)$total_reads <- colSums(assay(single_tse_ra, "relative_abundance"))

plotColData(
  single_tse_ra,
  y = "total_reads",
  x = "study_condition",
  colour_by = "study_condition"
)

All three groups cluster tightly around 100%, confirming no systematic QC bias between them.

Alpha diversity: within-sample richness

Shannon diversity captures how evenly and richly the microbial community is represented within each individual sample.

single_tse_ra <- addAlpha(
  single_tse_ra,
  assay.type = "relative_abundance",
  index = "shannon"
)

plotColData(
  single_tse_ra,
  y = "shannon",
  x = "study_condition",
  colour_by = "study_condition"
)

Diversity is broadly similar across all three groups — consistent with the CRC literature, where disease-associated microbiome shifts are usually driven by specific taxa rather than an overall loss of diversity.

Beta diversity: between-sample community structure

Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, visualised via Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA), shows whether samples cluster by disease status based on overall community composition.

plotReducedDim(

Substantial overlap between groups — again pointing toward taxon-specific signals rather than a wholesale community shift, which is exactly what the differential abundance methods below are designed to detect.

Differential Abundance: LEfSe

A scaling note

lefser() internally expects abundances scaled to sum to 1,000,000 per sample (parts-per-million), a convention inherited from the original LEfSe tool. Its own lefser:::relativeAb() function applies this scaling correctly — general-purpose relative-abundance transforms (e.g. from mia, which scale to 0–1) are not equivalent here and will cause the LDA effect-size step to silently return no results.

single_ra_adenoma <- lefser:::relativeAb(
  single_tse[, single_tse$study_condition %in% c("control", "adenoma")]
)
single_ra_adenoma$study_condition <- droplevels(
  as.factor(single_ra_adenoma$study_condition)
)

res_lefse_adenoma <- lefser(
  single_ra_adenoma,
  classCol = "study_condition",
  lda.threshold = 3,
  trim.names = TRUE
)
lefserPlot(res_lefse_adenoma)

Summary & Interpretation

Key findings

  • Community-level structure (alpha and beta diversity) does not differ dramatically across control, adenoma, and CRC — the microbiome is not globally disrupted at either disease stage.
  • Taxon-level differences are substantial: ANCOM-BC2 identified 21 significant taxa in adenoma vs. control and 38 in CRC vs. control, showing a clear escalation with disease progression.
  • LEfSe independently corroborates several of the same taxa, including CRC-associated enrichment of Fusobacterium and Escherichia coli — consistent with prior literature on CRC-microbiome associations.
  • Together, these results support the idea that specific bacterial taxa, rather than overall community diversity, are the more sensitive signal for CRC and its precursor lesions.

Question

How can we perform a multi-group LEfSe analysis to identify differentially abundant features across the three conditions simultaneously?

Next steps

  • Extend this analysis to additional CRC cohorts (e.g. YachidaS_2019, ThomasAM_2018a, FengQ_2015) to test whether these taxa replicate across independent populations
  • Adjust for study-level batch effects when pooling multiple cohorts
  • Cross-reference significant taxa against curated microbiome signature databases (e.g. BugSigDB) for independent validation
